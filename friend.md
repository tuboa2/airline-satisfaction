# Closing the ROC-AUC Gap on Kaggle S6E10: A Synergistic Approach Combining Density Forensics, Orthogonal Neural Networks, and Transductive Learning

## Domain A: Distribution-Agnostic Density and Frequency Forensics for Synthetic Data Exploitation

The objective of this domain is to identify and exploit subtle, non-obvious signals within the synthetically generated Kaggle Playground Series S6E10 dataset that are missed by standard Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDTs). The user has explicitly directed a focus away from complex latent space reverse-engineering of the generative model (e.g., CTGAN, TVAE) towards more robust, distribution-agnostic forensic methods . This strategic choice is grounded in the "Black-Box Approximator Trap," where attempting to reconstruct the generator's internal mechanics introduces significant computational overhead and approximation errors, particularly on constrained Kaggle T4 GPU environments [[9](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2025-037), [29](https://www.kaggle.com/c/playground-series-s6e7/writeups/29th-place-ft-transformer-exact-value-target-en)]. Instead, the approach prioritizes direct measurement of the artifacts produced by the generator, such as mode collapse and discrete density pooling, which manifest as statistical anomalies in the final dataset . The confirmed Benford's Law deviations serve as a primary indicator of these underlying generator artifacts, suggesting that the numerical values are not drawn from a perfectly natural distribution but rather from a structured, albeit artificial, process [[1](https://medium.com/ds3ucsd/unveiling-the-power-of-benford's-law-and-how-it's-revolutionizing-data-science-875bac4d562c)]. This section details three key methodologies designed to capture these artifacts: High-Order Categorical Frequency Encoding, k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) Distance Features, and Local Outlier Factor (LOF) scoring. Each method provides a unique lens through which to view the data's structure, offering signals that are fundamentally different from the axis-aligned splits characteristic of tree-based models. These features aim to provide the model with information about the "typicality" or "density" of a passenger's profile, which may correlate strongly with satisfaction probability in a way that is difficult for GBDTs to approximate directly.

The first proposed method, High-Order Categorical Frequency Encoding, targets the interactions between multiple categorical variables. While one-hot encoding is a common technique for handling categorical data, it creates a sparse, high-dimensional feature space that can be inefficient and prone to overfitting, especially with high-cardinality features [[3](https://suparnachowdhury.medium.com/the-art-of-feature-engineering-encoding-categorical-variables-ee180c5b2b93)]. An alternative, frequency encoding, replaces each category with its relative frequency in the dataset, which can be more robust and preserves information about how common or rare a particular category is [[3](https://suparnachowdhury.medium.com/the-art-of-feature-engineering-encoding-categorical-variables-ee180c5b2b93), [7](https://blog.dailydoseofds.com/p/7-categorical-data-encoding-techniques-1f5)]. However, this still fails to capture the joint distribution of categories. The synthetic generation process likely samples from a discrete probability distribution over all possible combinations of key features like `Class`, `Type of Travel`, and top survey items (`Inflight wifi`, `Online boarding`, etc.). Rare combinations may be undersampled, creating low-density regions in the feature space, while common ones are oversampled. By calculating the frequency of these high-order crosses, we create a new numerical feature that acts as a direct proxy for the "sampling density" of a given passenger profile. The hypothesis is that GBDTs, which partition the feature space into rectangular regions, will struggle to model the smooth variations in satisfaction probability associated with these density changes, especially if the relationship is non-linear. Providing this frequency as a direct input gives the model a powerful signal about how typical or anomalous a passenger's combination of traits is. This method is computationally efficient, leveraging fast pandas operations that can be accelerated on GPUs, making it highly feasible within the Kaggle environment [[18](https://medium.com/nvidia-merlin/faster-gpu-based-feature-engineering-and-tabular-deep-learning-training-with-nvtabular-on-kaggle-com-9791fa2f4b61)].

The second method, k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) Distance Features, addresses the geometric structure of the data manifold. Tree models create piecewise-constant predictions within hyper-rectangular partitions of the feature space. They are inherently poor at capturing the smooth, continuous nature of class probability changes near decision boundaries or within clusters of points. In contrast, k-NN provides a non-parametric measure of local density and proximity to class centroids. The core idea is that the distance of a given passenger to the centers of gravity for satisfied and dissatisfied passengers should correlate with their true satisfaction probability. Furthermore, areas of abnormally large distances to neighbors correspond to potential artifacts of the generative model, such as mode collapse. By calculating the distance to the nearest `satisfaction=1` and `satisfaction=0` neighbors, we provide the model with a powerful, non-linear signal that is structurally orthogonal to GBDT splits. To implement this, all numerical features must be normalized, and categorical features must be encoded into a numerical format (e.g., label encoding or entity embeddings) to create a unified representation for distance calculation [[35](https://metricgate.com/docs/local-outlier-factor-lof/)]. Efficient KD-Tree or Ball-Tree implementations of k-NN should be used for scalability [[34](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.neighbors.LocalOutlierFactor.html)]. For each point, we calculate the average distance to its K-nearest neighbors within each class. Derived features, such as the logarithm of these distances or their ratio, can help stabilize the signal and make it more interpretable for downstream models. This process is performed on the combined train+test set to ensure consistent feature distributions, a practice that is permissible in a transductive setting [[23](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-025-11326-3), [24](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.13796v2)]. This method directly targets the identified manifold boundary interactions in the empirical baseline and holds significant potential for lift .

The third and final method for Domain A is the application of the Local Outlier Factor (LOF) algorithm. LOF is a density-based anomaly detection method that measures the local deviation of a data point's density relative to its neighbors [[31](https://pub.towardsai.net/an-in-depth-guide-to-local-outlier-factor-lof-for-outlier-detection-in-python-5a6f128e5871), [33](https://medium.com/dataman-in-ai/handbook-of-anomaly-detection-9-local-outlier-factor-lof-6a415e8bd873)]. It quantifies how isolated a point is by comparing its local reachability density to the densities of its k-nearest neighbors [[32](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-2289/5/1/1), [35](https://metricgate.com/docs/local-outlier-factor-lof/)]. A high LOF score indicates that a point has a significantly lower density than its neighbors, flagging it as a potential outlier. The hypothesis here is that mode collapse in the generative model manifests as regions of abnormally low density in the feature space [[13](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/generating-synthetic-data-with-transformers-a-solution-for-enterprise-data-challenges/)]. Passengers whose feature vectors fall into these low-density regions are likely to have been generated by a collapsed mode and represent a distinct class of outliers. Since GBDTs are optimized for separating dense, well-defined manifolds, they will naturally perform poorly on these isolated, low-density points. By providing the LOF score as a feature, we allow the model to specifically attend to these anomalous instances and adjust its predictions accordingly. The implementation involves standardizing all features before fitting the LOF estimator, as this ensures the distance metric is meaningful [[35](https://metricgate.com/docs/local-outlier-factor-lof/)]. Using scikit-learn's `LocalOutlierFactor` with the `contamination='auto'` parameter allows the algorithm to estimate the fraction of outliers in the dataset automatically [[34](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.neighbors.LocalOutlierFactor.html)]. The resulting outlier scores, available via the `negative_outlier_factor_` attribute, can be used directly as a feature in the final model. This method is a medium-risk, potentially high-reward addition, as it directly probes for the "mode duplication" issues hinted at in the user's rationale .

## Domain D: Orthogonalizing Ensembles with Compact FT-Transformer Architectures

To close the remaining ROC-AUC gap, the research mandates moving beyond the current elite GBDT performance (~0.9588 OOF) by introducing a model with structurally diverse and orthogonal decision boundaries . The core principle guiding this effort is that ensembling models with uncorrelated error patterns yields greater performance gains than blending similar models. While two GBDT variants like LightGBM and XGBoost might exhibit a residual correlation of ~0.985, a well-designed neural network could achieve a much lower correlation of ~0.920, leading to a more substantial boost when blended . The user's directive is explicit: the goal is not to build a standalone deep learning model that beats the GBDT baseline, but to construct a model that scores in the range of 0.9540–0.9565 standalone. This model's value lies entirely in its ability to correct the specific errors made by the GBDT ensemble, thereby maximizing the overall ensemble's performance . This requires an architecture capable of modeling relational patterns and complex, non-linear interactions in the tabular data—capabilities that are fundamentally different from the recursive partitioning strategy of GBDTs.

The recommended architecture for this task is a compact FT-Transformer (Feature Tokenizer Transformer) [[10](https://towardsdatascience.com/improving-tabtransformer-part-1-linear-numerical-embeddings-dbc3be3b5bb5/)]. The FT-Transformer is a powerful deep learning model adapted from NLP Transformers for tabular data. Unlike earlier transformer-based models like TabTransformer that treated numerical features differently, the FT-Transformer embeds both numerical and categorical features into a shared embedding space and processes them jointly within the Transformer blocks [[10](https://towardsdatascience.com/improving-tabtransformer-part-1-linear-numerical-embeddings-dbc3be3b5bb5/)]. This design allows it to learn intricate inter-feature relationships and dependencies that are invisible to tree-based models. GBDTs partition the feature space using axis-aligned hyperplanes, resulting in a series of rectangular decision boundaries. In contrast, the FT-Transformer learns a smooth, continuous function through gradient descent and multi-head self-attention. Its decision boundary is defined by a complex, often hyperspherical, manifold in the learned embedding space. This fundamental difference in how the feature space is modeled is precisely what creates the desired orthogonal residual errors. When a GBDT makes an error in a region of overlapping classes, the FT-Transformer, having learned a different underlying structure, is likely to make a different error, thus correcting the ensemble's mistake.

The architectural specification must be optimized for both performance and speed on Kaggle T4 infrastructure. The goal is to complete a 5-fold cross-validation run in under 30 minutes per run . A larger, more complex model might offer higher standalone accuracy but would violate this critical constraint. Therefore, a compact configuration is essential. The following specifications are proposed:

1.  **Categorical Feature Processing:** For each categorical feature, an entity embedding is used. An entity embedding is a lookup table that maps each category ID to a dense vector. The dimension of this vector should be `min(64, (num_categories + 1) // 2)` [[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16109v1)]. This dimensionality reduction is crucial for managing memory and preventing the curse of dimensionality that plagues one-hot encoding, especially for features with many unique values [[3](https://suparnachowdhury.medium.com/the-art-of-feature-engineering-encoding-categorical-variables-ee180c5b2b93)].

2.  **Numerical Feature Processing:** Each numerical feature is passed through a dedicated Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). A simple MLP with one hidden layer can project the single numerical value into an embedding vector of the same dimension as the categorical embeddings. This aligns the representation spaces so they can be concatenated and processed together [[10](https://towardsdatascience.com/improving-tabtransformer-part-1-linear-numerical-embeddings-dbc3be3b5bb5/)].

3.  **Transformer Core:** The core of the model consists of a stack of Transformer encoder blocks. For this use case, 2 to 3 blocks are sufficient. Each block should contain 4 attention heads. Using fewer layers and heads reduces the model's parameter count and computational complexity, which is vital for fast convergence on the T4 GPU [[11](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/13/7262), [20](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.14013v3)]. The hidden dimension for the embeddings should match the total dimension of the concatenated categorical and numerical embeddings.

4.  **Classification Head:** Following the Transformer blocks, a special `[CLS]` token is appended to the sequence of feature embeddings [[10](https://towardsdatascience.com/improving-tabtransformer-part-1-linear-numerical-embeddings-dbc3be3b5bb5/)]. The final contextualized embedding of this `[CLS]` token is then passed to a small, shallow MLP classifier head (e.g., two linear layers with dropout and ReLU activation) to produce the final binary prediction for satisfaction. This mechanism is analogous to the `[CLS]` token used in NLP tasks to aggregate information for classification.

This compact FT-Transformer architecture is projected to meet the user's requirements. Training times for similar deep tabular models on comparable hardware (e.g., RTX 4090) are reported to be in the range of 9-13 minutes per fold [[11](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/13/7262)]. Given that the Kaggle T4 GPU has strong FP16 performance (~65 TFLOPS) due to its Tensor Cores, and considering the smaller batch sizes and simpler architecture, a 5-fold run in under 30 minutes is a realistic expectation [[30](https://medium.com/@speaktoharisudhan/multi-gpu-training-in-kaggle-harnessing-2-t4-for-faster-model-training-tensorflow-f091c22db99e)]. The model's standalone performance is not the primary metric; its role is to serve as a diverse learner. Once trained, its predictions will be blended with the GBDT ensemble. Due to the orthogonal error hypothesis, this blend is expected to yield a significant lift, likely accounting for a large portion of the required 0.0032 ROC-AUC improvement. The explainability offered by analyzing attention weights further adds value, allowing for introspection into the model's decision-making process [[10](https://towardsdatascience.com/improving-tabtransformer-part-1-linear-numerical-embeddings-dbc3be3b5bb5/)].

## Domain C: Safe Transductive Learning and Consistency Regularization with Full Test Set Utilization

The third pillar of the research strategy focuses on leveraging the full unlabeled test set (~300,000 rows) to enhance model performance, guided by the principles of safe, transductive learning . The user correctly identifies that since the train and test sets are independent random draws from the same synthetic data generator, certain operations that would typically constitute data leakage are permissible in a transductive setting [[23](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-025-11326-3), [24](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.13796v2)]. Transductive learning aims for high performance on a specific, pre-existing set of data rather than generalization to new, unseen instances. This paradigm shift allows for the use of information from the entire available dataset during training, including for feature engineering and model calibration, without the risk of information leakage that would occur in an inductive setup [[23](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-025-11326-3)]. The directive specifies a "medium risk tolerance," favoring sophisticated semi-supervised techniques like soft pseudo-labeling and teacher-student distillation over naive hard-thresholding strategies that could overfit to Public Leaderboard artifacts . This section outlines a methodology combining transductive feature engineering with a robust teacher-student distillation framework to safely exploit the test set and close the final ROC-AUC gap.

The foundational step is transductive feature engineering on the combined `train + test` dataset. As established in Domain A, features like High-Order Categorical Frequency Encoding and k-NN Distance Features are created using statistics calculated on the full dataset. This practice is considered safe because the goal is not to generalize to new data but to build the most accurate model possible for the provided evaluation set. Performing these calculations on the combined data ensures that the feature distributions are consistent between the training and test sets, reducing potential distribution shift and leading to more stable and reliable Out-of-Fold (OOF) predictions. This includes any form of target encoding where smoothing parameters are calculated globally. A benchmark study concluded that regularized versions of target encoding consistently provide the best results, often being faster to train subsequent algorithms due to producing more efficient representations [[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00180-022-01207-6)]. This initial step stabilizes the modeling pipeline and prepares the ground for more advanced semi-supervised techniques.

The core of the transductive strategy is a teacher-student distillation framework. In this setup, the "teacher" is the highly-performing GBDT ensemble (or a single LightGBM model trained on the full training set), and the "student" is the newly introduced Compact FT-Transformer from Domain D. Knowledge distillation is a meta-learning technique where knowledge from a large, complex model (the teacher) is transferred to a smaller, simpler model (the student) [[22](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/soft-labeling-advances)]. Instead of training the student on hard, one-hot encoded labels (0 or 1), it is trained on the soft probabilities output by the teacher. These probabilities convey not only the predicted class but also the model's confidence in that prediction, providing a richer learning signal. The hypothesis is that the GBDT ensemble, having been trained on all available labeled data, has already learned a very robust and nuanced decision boundary. By training the FT-Transformer to mimic this boundary, we regularize the neural network and guide it towards a high-quality solution, improving its standalone performance and ensuring its errors remain somewhat aligned with the GBDT's. However, because the FT-Transformer uses a completely different optimization landscape (gradient descent on a continuous function vs. boosting trees), it will inevitably make different mistakes, preserving the orthogonality needed for a successful final blend.

To incorporate the unlabeled test set, the framework extends to semi-supervised learning via soft pseudo-labeling. First, the trained teacher model generates soft probabilities (class 1 likelihood) for every row in the unlabeled test set. These probabilities are not used as hard 0/1 labels; instead, they serve as "soft targets" for the student model during its training phase. The loss function for the student becomes a weighted sum of two components: the standard cross-entropy loss on the original labeled training data, and a consistency regularization term on the pseudo-labeled test data [[14](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data/)]. This second term penalizes the student if its own prediction for a test sample deviates significantly from the teacher's assigned soft probability for that sample. This encourages the model to make confident and stable predictions on the test set, effectively pulling the decision boundary away from ambiguous regions. This approach is more robust than hard thresholding, as it avoids the brittle 0.5 probability cutoff and instead leverages the calibrated uncertainty estimates of the teacher model. Recent Kaggle solutions have successfully employed soft pseudo-labeling to great effect, demonstrating its utility in competitive settings [[21](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/nbme-score-clinical-patient-notes/discussion/322832)]. To manage risk and prevent confirmation bias, curriculum-based pseudo-labeling can be implemented, where only the highest-confidence test-set predictions are included in the regularization term, and the set of pseudo-labeled samples is gradually expanded over training epochs [[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.14013v3)]. This systematic approach safely leverages the vast amount of unlabeled data to refine the student model's decision boundary, directly targeting the final ROC-AUC lift required to reach the rank-one threshold.

## Strategic Ensemble Synthesis and Final Recommendations

To bridge the final ~0.0032 ROC-AUC gap to achieve a rank-one score of 0.96137+, a synergistic strategy integrating the methodologies from Domains A, C, and D is required. The current elite GBDT baseline, with its verified OOF ROC-AUC of approximately 0.9588, has already captured the most dominant signals in the data . Closing the remaining gap necessitates the introduction of models and features that target subtler, more complex patterns that are orthogonal to the GBDT's decision-making process. This final section synthesizes the proposed methods into a coherent execution plan, culminating in a final ensemble strategy designed to maximize performance lift while adhering to the strict computational and risk constraints of the Kaggle environment.

The recommended execution plan proceeds in three sequential phases. First, the foundation of the feature space must be enhanced by implementing the distribution-agnostic forensic techniques from Domain A. The initial priority should be the implementation of **High-Order Categorical Frequency Encoding** and **k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) Distance Features**. These methods are computationally inexpensive, do not require assumptions about the underlying generative model, and directly address the known synthetic artifacts, such as mode collapse and density pooling . High-Order Categorical Frequency Encoding will provide a direct signal of a passenger's "typicality" based on their unique combination of traits, a signal that is difficult for GBDTs to model effectively [[3](https://suparnachowdhury.medium.com/the-art-of-feature-engineering-encoding-categorical-variables-ee180c5b2b93), [7](https://blog.dailydoseofds.com/p/7-categorical-data-encoding-techniques-1f5)]. The k-NN Distance Features will provide a powerful geometric signal related to the passenger's proximity to pure classes of satisfied and dissatisfied individuals, directly probing the manifold boundary interactions previously identified . These two features alone are expected to contribute a modest but significant lift, likely closing a portion of the initial gap. Following this, the **Local Outlier Factor (LOF)** feature should be implemented as a secondary option. LOF serves as a targeted probe for the "mode duplication" artifacts mentioned in the user's rationale, potentially identifying a class of outliers that GBDTs misclassify [[13](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/generating-synthetic-data-with-transformers-a-solution-for-enterprise-data-challenges/), [31](https://pub.towardsai.net/an-in-depth-guide-to-local-outlier-factor-lof-for-outlier-detection-in-python-5a6f128e5871)].

Second, the diverse deep learning architecture from Domain D must be developed and integrated. The construction of a **Compact FT-Transformer** with entity embeddings and a minimal number of Transformer blocks is the key action item here [[10](https://towardsdatascience.com/improving-tabtransformer-part-1-linear-numerical-embeddings-dbc3be3b5bb5/)]. The primary objective during this phase is not to achieve the highest possible standalone ROC-AUC score, but to ensure the model learns a fundamentally different set of patterns from the GBDTs. This is achieved by leveraging the Transformer's ability to model complex, relational features within the attention mechanism, a capability entirely absent in tree-based models [[11](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/13/7262)]. The training process should be closely monitored to ensure it converges within the specified time constraints on a Kaggle T4 GPU . The resulting model will serve as the cornerstone of the orthogonal component of the final ensemble.

Third, the transductive learning framework from Domain C must be executed to fully leverage the unlabeled test set. This begins with comprehensive **Transductive Feature Engineering**, where all relevant statistics are calculated on the combined train+test dataset to ensure distributional stability [[23](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-025-11326-3)]. Following this, the **Teacher-Student Soft Distillation** framework is implemented. The strong GBDT ensemble acts as the teacher, providing soft probability targets to guide the training of the FT-Transformer student. Crucially, this is augmented with consistency regularization on the soft pseudo-labels generated for the entire test set. This powerful semi-supervised approach will refine the student model's decision boundary, forcing it to be more confident and robust in the unlabeled regions of the feature space, without the risks associated with hard-thresholding [[14](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data/), [20](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.14013v3)]. This step is designed to push the standalone performance of the FT-Transformer and, more importantly, ensure its error patterns are sufficiently uncorrelated with those of the GBDT ensemble.

The final step is the synthesis of the predictions from the diverse models into a single, superior forecast. The optimal blending strategy will combine the out-of-fold (OOF) predictions from the GBDT ensemble with the OOF predictions from the distilled FT-Transformer. Due to the carefully engineered structural differences between the two models—the piecewise-constant functions of the GBDTs versus the smooth, continuous function of the FT-Transformer—their residual prediction errors are expected to be highly uncorrelated . Simple weighted averaging is often effective in such scenarios. The weights can be determined empirically through a grid search or by using a simple hill-climbing optimization on the validation set to find the combination that maximizes the ensemble's ROC-AUC. Given that blending two models with a residual correlation of around 0.920 can yield a significant boost, it is highly probable that this final ensemble will successfully close the remaining ~0.0032 ROC-AUC gap, propelling the submission to the top of the leaderboard. This multi-pronged approach—enhancing the feature space, introducing a structurally diverse learner, and safely exploiting the full dataset—is the most promising path forward to achieving the research goal.
